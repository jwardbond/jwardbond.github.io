I don't remember where I saw this book recommended, or why---despite my reservations that a book about evictions might be a bit dull---I added it to my to-read list at all. If every other book on my e-reader hadn't been formattedallwonky, and if I wasn't on a trans-pacific flight where the only other reading material was a single-page comic about _10 things to remember before you die in a horrible accident_, I probably never would have picked this book up at all. What a travesty that would have been.

I think part of the problem was that I didn't understand what "ethnographies" are, or what their purpose is. The word brought to mind subtitles from the writings of a sociology grad student, and not much else. Although I recognized vaguely that such an approach must be interesting to some people, I didn't see how it would be a useful way of understanding a structural, quantifiable problem like mass evictions. Especially given the fact that this particular ethnography was only concerned with a couple families in Milwaukee, whereas evictions are a global problem (phenomenon?).

Although it probably comes as no surprise to the thousands of you that read my reviews, it was a bit of a revelation to me when I realized that the main purpose of an ethnography (at least in this case) is to build the empathy necessary to contextualize the statistics. By spending years embedding himself with the Milwaukee families both recieving and delivering evictions, Matthew Desmond was able to build (and transmit to the reader) an incredibly _human_ story.

All this context doesn't even come at the cost of "the big picture" because the author does such a good job giving you one-two punches of individual experience and heartbreaking statistics.

---

And it _is_ a heartbreaking book. The people in it are often at the end of the road, swaying right at the edge of the cliff between housed and homeless. They are fighting so hard to not slip off the precarious edge, but everything in the world is conspiring to push them off: they are one sick day away from losing their job, one missed rent payment (with interest) away from losing their home, a couple missed storage payments (with interest) away from having the moving company that evicted them (because they couldn't pay rent) auction off their belongings, one missed case-worker appointment from losing their benefits, one missed court date from being evicted.

This book is also infuriating. There are more catch 22s in here than there are in the book Catch 22 itself. They can chose to take a day off work to fight their evictions, but then they might lose the job they need to pay rent. They can accept the eviction and move, but they might end up being much farther from work and losing their jobs. Often the move costs them their job, their social and support networks, their belongings, their kids' educational stability, all of which makes it harder to keep paying rent. They sometimes get evicted _for fucking nothing_: if their kids are too loud during the day because they aren't in school, if an ex-boyfriend makes trouble trying to get in the building, if they complain about their living conditions to the landlord or to the city's safety inspectors. So many things are out of their control, and they every time they slip the just get kicked and kicked and kicked and kicked and kicked. It makes you so angry, the 9th time you read about a landlord evicting a single mom bbecause she couldn't make rent and feed her kids at the same time.
